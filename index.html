Story: “The Heavy Suitcase”

Marco is flying from Genoa to London for the first time on his own. He arrives at the airport two hours before his flight and looks for the check-in desk of his airline. The queue is short, and soon the agent says, “Good morning. May I see your passport, please?”

Then she asks him to place his suitcase on the scale. It shows 24 kilos, but the limit is 23. Marco has to decide: he can pay an overweight fee, or he can move a pair of shoes and a jacket into his carry-on. He puts on the jacket, packs the shoes in his backpack, and smiles when the scale shows 22.8.

“Did you pack your bags yourself?” the agent asks. “Yes, and they’ve been with me the whole time.” She prints his boarding pass and smiles. “Gate B12, boarding at 10:15. Have a nice flight, Mr. Rossi.”

Think about it: What would you do in Marco’s place? What is the difference between a carry-on and a checked bag?

From home to the gate: the check-in journey.

At the desk: the 6 essential steps

  1. Locate the check-in desk or terminal counter of your airline.
  2. Present your passport so the agent can verify your details.
  3. Check and weigh your luggage on the scale (extra or overweight bags may cost a fee).
  4. Answer the security questions: who packed your bags, and did you leave them unattended?
  5. Choose your seat if requested: aisle, middle or window.
  6. Collect your boarding pass, baggage tags and flight information (gate, seat, boarding time).

Carry-on or checked?

Bags you keep with you are carry-on bags. Bags that go into the cargo compartment are checked bags.

Fun fact

Luggage is more common in British English, baggage in American English and in airline announcements (“baggage claim”). Both are uncountable.

When should I arrive?

For a domestic flight, arrive about two hours before departure. For an international flight, plan for three to four hours, because of immigration and longer security lines.

Story: “The Water Bottle”

Giulia arrives at the security check with a full bottle of water in her bag and a big tube of hand cream in her pocket. The officer points at a sign: liquids must be in containers of 100 ml or less, inside a clear plastic bag. “Is it just water?” Giulia asks. “I’m afraid the size of the bottle is the problem, madam, not what is inside.”

Giulia looks around. The queue is long, and her flight is boarding in forty minutes. So she drinks half the water in front of everyone and throws the rest away. Then she puts her bag, her laptop and her jacket into a tray, takes off her belt, and walks through the scanner. It beeps. She had forgotten a small pair of scissors in her jacket. “Those are restricted items,” the officer says kindly.

Twenty minutes later, Giulia is finally at the gate, with an empty bottle in her hand. The screen says: DELAYED. She laughs and walks to the water fountain. “Next time,” she thinks, “I’ll buy my water after security.”

Think about it: What went wrong for Giulia, and what could she do differently next time?

At the security check

  1. Take out your laptop, tablet and liquids from your bag.
  2. Put your bag, jacket and belt in a tray.
  3. Empty your pockets: keys, coins, phone.
  4. Walk through the scanner or metal detector.
  5. Wait if the officer asks you to open your bag or does a pat-down.
  6. Collect your things and put your belt and shoes back on.

The liquids rule

In most airports, each liquid (water, perfume, cream, gel) must be in a container of 100 ml or less, and all the containers must fit in one clear plastic bag. It is the size of the container that counts, not how much is left inside. You can buy drinks after security. Rules can change and differ between airports: always check your airport’s website before you travel.

Understanding announcements

Patterns you will hear

  • Boarding: “Flight BA 603 to London is now boarding at gate 14.”
  • Final call: “This is the final call for passengers travelling to Madrid.”
  • Delay: “We regret to inform you that… has been delayed.”
  • Change: “The gate has been changed from B4 to B9.”
  • Instructions: “Passengers are requested to…” / “Please proceed to…”

Tip: catch three things first: flight number, destination, gate. Then listen for the problem.

Story: “Turbulence over the Alps”

Elena hates turbulence. Over the Alps the plane starts to shake, and the seat belt sign comes on. The captain’s calm voice fills the cabin: “Ladies and gentlemen, we are flying through some turbulence. Please stay seated and keep your seat belt fastened.”

Elena presses the call light and asks the flight attendant for a glass of water. He smiles. “It’s completely normal,” he says. “In fifteen minutes it will be smooth again.” He also helps the man next to her, who cannot fit his bag in the overhead compartment.

Fifteen minutes later, the plane is quiet. Elena puts on her earphones, closes the window shade and, for the first time, falls asleep before the descent.

Think about it: Why does the flight attendant smile? What would you ask the crew for if you felt nervous?

Story: “The Lost Wallet”

In Barcelona, Francesca realises her wallet is gone. She stays calm and thinks. Where was she? At the market, at the café, on the metro. She retraces her steps, but there is no sign of it.

She goes to the police station. “Excuse me, I’d like to report a stolen wallet.” The officer takes her details and gives her a report number. Then she calls her bank to block her card and tells her insurance company.

The next morning, a café owner calls: someone found the wallet and left it at the bar. Everything is inside, except the coffee loyalty card. Francesca laughs with relief, and decides to always keep a copy of her documents.

Think about it: What did Francesca do right? Which two things would you do first if your wallet was stolen?

Emergency numbers

Health problems

PharmacistHow long have you had it?
TouristSince yesterday.

Grammar spotlight: since and for

Since + a point in time: since Monday. For + a period: for two days. Use the present perfect: “I’ve had a fever since yesterday” / “for two days”.
